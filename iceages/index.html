Orbital parameters, 1,000 to 3 million years ago: Berger, A. & Loutre, M. F. (1991). Insolation values for the climate of the last 10 million years. Quaternary Science Reviews 10, 297–317 (NOAA NCEI Paleoclimatology, orbit91). Today and the next 100,000 years: Berger, A. (1978). Long-term variations of daily insolation and Quaternary climatic changes. Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences 35, 2362–2367 (NOAA NCEI, bein1 and bein11). The two solutions differ slightly where they meet (eccentricity by about 0.0005). Sunlight calculated from these parameters with a solar constant of 1360 W/m²; dates use a calendar fixed to the March equinox (March 20). Total summer energy: sunlight summed over all days with more than 275 W/m², after Huybers, P. (2006). Early Pleistocene glacial cycles and the integrated summer insolation forcing. Science 313, 508–511.

CO₂: ice cores, Bereiter, B. et al. (2015). Revision of the EPICA Dome C CO₂ record from 800 to 600 kyr before present. Geophysical Research Letters, doi:10.1002/2014GL061957 (composite record, NOAA NCEI). Boron-isotope estimates (dots, ±1σ): Dyez, K. A., Hönisch, B. & Schmidt, G. A. (2018). Early Pleistocene obliquity-scale pCO₂ variability at ~1.5 million years ago. Paleoceanography and Paleoclimatology, doi:10.1029/2018PA003349; de la Vega, E. et al. (2020). Atmospheric CO₂ during the Mid-Piacenzian Warm Period and the M2 glaciation. Scientific Reports 10, 11002; Martínez-Botí, M. A. et al. (2015). Plio-Pleistocene climate sensitivity evaluated using high-resolution CO₂ records. Nature 518, 49–54. Values as recalculated in the Paleo-CO₂ (CenCO₂PIP) database, highest-quality category only (NOAA NCEI). The ice-core record ends in 2001 (368 ppm); today’s value is about 420 ppm.

Dust: dust mass accumulation rate at ODP Site 1090, subantarctic Atlantic. Martínez-García, A. et al. (2011). Southern Ocean dust–climate coupling over the past four million years. Nature 476, 312–316 (data: PANGAEA, doi:10.1594/PANGAEA.767458, CC-BY 3.0).

Ice volume: benthic foraminifera δ¹⁸O stack, Lisiecki, L. E. & Raymo, M. E. (2005). A Pliocene-Pleistocene stack of 57 globally distributed benthic δ¹⁸O records. Paleoceanography 20, PA1003. Higher δ¹⁸O means more ice on land and colder deep water. Rate of ice loss: change in δ¹⁸O per 1,000 years after a 5,000-year running mean. Note that the LR04 timescale was partly tuned to orbital forcing.
